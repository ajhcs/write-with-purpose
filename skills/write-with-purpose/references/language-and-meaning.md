# Language and meaning

Use this reference when wording, grouping, or order could change the reader's interpretation. Resolve the intended relationship from the evidence and context before choosing a sentence construction.

## Make the actors and actions recoverable

Name the actor, action, and affected party where the reader needs them. Use active voice when an evidenced actor matters. Passive voice can keep attention on the affected object or accommodate an unknown actor. Give a pronoun a clear antecedent or repeat a precise term. Distinguish an entity from its property, a process from its result, and a record from the event it describes.

For a proposed change, name what happens now, who or what would act differently, and the result the reader can look for. Use a technical term when it is precise, then explain the relation the reader needs. An economical connective can show a supported inference. The evidence still needs to establish the link.

An argument that “stayed with me from” a report can be described as memorable without claiming the author first encountered it there. When an author corrects a disclaimer, follow the correction at its supported scope and keep any independent qualifications that still apply. Preserve useful improvements around the corrected statement.

## Place qualifications where they apply

Keep a modifier close to the words it governs. Check what “only,” “all,” “each,” and “not” apply to, and whether a condition at the end of a series governs one item or every item. Repeat the operative word when it resolves a real ambiguity. Give an introductory participial phrase its intended actor. Place a prerequisite before the action if the reader must establish it first.

Preserve whether a statement conveys possibility, ability, permission, expectation, recommendation, or obligation. Keep a date or duration attached to the relevant observation, record, event, or deadline. Put any conditions and bounds the comparison needs where the reader will encounter them.

## Group what belongs together

Use punctuation and placement to show whether a clause identifies which case you mean, adds secondary information, or carries a necessary condition. Keep conditions, reasons, and examples attached to the claims they serve. For lists, make sure each item fits the introduction and that distinct required steps survive. Choose one developed example when several illustrate the same relationship and the brief allows selection.

When a phrase obscures what happens, use the supported action. In a passage about the cost of a proposed repair, “priced out of urgency” can mean the request was “set aside because a fix seems too expensive.” That reading depends on the source and leaves the cost judgment visible.

Read the paragraph for what each sentence prepares. A brief definition near its term, a shared subject for related clauses, or a clear inference between sentences can keep the thought moving. Choose sentence boundaries that carry the thought without obscuring it. Reread surrounding text after cutting a clause or moving information so the reader can still find the premise or reference they need.
