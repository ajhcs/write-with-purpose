# Language and meaning

Use this reference to examine a construction whose interpretation matters. Establish the intended relation from the source and context. Resolve a consequential competing reading; a grammatical label alone gives no reason to change a sentence.

## Actors and reference

Connect an action to its actor, object and affected party. Active voice often makes responsibility visible; passive voice can preserve an unknown actor or keep attention on the affected object. An active rewrite needs an evidenced actor.

Use a pronoun when its referent is clear. Repeat a precise noun when nearby nouns compete. Keep an entity distinct from its property, a process from its result, and a record from the event it describes. Stable names help the reader follow these relations.

## Evidence, inference and action

When a conclusion depends on a relationship, supply the link between the stated facts and the judgment. A useful framing sentence can orient the reader before the particulars. Keep precise terms for the relevant concepts and use plain clauses to explain their relationship: what each refers to, how one affects another, or why a fact supports the judgment. A connective can expose that link; it cannot supply missing evidence for it.

For a proposed change, identify what currently happens and what should happen instead. State the affected object, action and relevant completion condition. A compressed label for the change is useful when the reader can recover the concrete instruction from it. If the reader must reconstruct that instruction, write it directly.

## Scope and attachment

Place a modifier where its scope is recoverable. Check what words such as only, all, each and not govern, and whether a final qualifier applies to one item or the whole series. Repeat a preposition, auxiliary or qualifier when needed to establish scope. Review plausible readings in context.

Introductory participial phrases need the intended implied actor. Attach subordinate conditions, reasons and limitations to the proposition they qualify. Clause order can change emphasis or the apparent relationship even when the words remain.

## Conditions and time

Distinguish a prerequisite, a condition, a recommendation and an action. Put a condition before an instruction when the reader needs it before acting. Preserve event order and whether the task requires establishing that a condition holds before proceeding.

Identify whether a time concerns observation, documentation, occurrence, deadline or duration when confusing them would matter. Bind a comparison to its relevant circumstances.

## Uncertainty and force

Preserve whether a statement describes possibility, ability, permission, expectation, recommendation or obligation. Familiar modal words can be exact. Retain negation, exceptions and bounds within the claim. Stronger wording needs stronger support.

## Punctuation and grouping

Choose punctuation for the relationship it expresses. Restrictive and supplementary clauses contribute different meanings. A colon can introduce an explanation or items; a semicolon can coordinate independent clauses or delimit complex series. Parentheses set secondary information outside the main line; a dash emphasizes a break. Choose the construction that makes the intended reading clear.

In a list, check the relationship between the introduction and each item. Distinguish required coverage from illustrative examples: trimming required steps can break an instruction, while one clear example can replace several that explain the same mechanism. Do not group people, tools, places and policies as though they occupy the same organizational role. Parallel form helps only when the relationships are actually parallel.

When a phrase obscures what happens, write the supported action directly. In an explanation about a proposed repair being rejected over cost, a phrase such as 'priced out of urgency' can become 'set aside because a fix seems too expensive.' This is a contextual interpretation, not a universal substitution rule. If several materially different meanings remain possible, resolve them from the source or identify the uncertainty.

## Paragraph connections

Give the reader enough continuity to follow the developing thought. Supply a causal link, contrast or inference where the argument needs it. A transition earns its place through that relationship. Similar wording can express different claims, while different wording can repeat one claim.

Place a brief definition in apposition or a subordinate clause when it can be read naturally with its term. Related clauses can share a subject or context instead of restarting the explanation. Choose sentence boundaries and lengths for the developing thought, and check the paragraph for choppy stops, overloaded joins and monotonous patterns.

Read the revision as a whole. Removing a clause can create a missing premise or an unclear reference elsewhere. Economical understanding includes the explanation needed to follow the reasoning.
