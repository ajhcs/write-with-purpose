## Assistant revision

After a hospital stay, for example, a community pharmacist may know what medications were dispensed but not what changed during admission or what the patient is actually taking. [Thornewill et al. (2022)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8572696/) identify person-centered, pharmacy-connected data sharing as one of four strategies for addressing nonoptimized medication therapy. Sharing discharge information and medication records across settings can help the care team adjust treatment to the patient’s health, preferences, and ability to take the medication. From a systems engineering perspective, the value of interoperability depends on how the connection supports people and their work across the care process. Shared terminology and accurate patient identification help systems interpret information consistently, while pharmacists, prescribers, and patients need ways to verify incomplete records and resolve discrepancies ([National Academy of Medicine, 2018](https://nam.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/Interop_508.pdf)).

Procuring interoperability means making information exchange a requirement throughout the purchase and use of healthcare technology. A hospital considering an electronic health record (EHR) or medical device should specify how it will securely send, receive, and use information with other systems. Contracts can require standard interfaces, transparent integration costs, data export, and testing for failed exchanges. The [National Academy of Medicine report](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/27114/chapter/6) proposes that providers combine their purchasing power, government support standards and testing, payers create incentives, and vendors demonstrate how their products work in clinical workflows. My [school telehealth design](https://colelyons.com/work/commonhealth-philadelphia-systems-engineering-portfolio/) reinforced why these details matter: a standard interface does not by itself establish who may access information or who resolves a failed handoff. Although that project was an academic proposal, its lesson applies to procurement: clinicians and patients should help define the work, and organizations should test whether information can be understood and acted on in daily care. The goal is for information to follow the patient in a form that supports care while protecting privacy.

I believe these approaches can work if organizations also invest in implementation and ongoing support. Shared requirements give vendors a reason to build compatible products, but smaller organizations may need help paying for interfaces, training, and maintenance. Workflows and payment arrangements must also give pharmacists time to review records and resolve discrepancies. Access alone does not ensure that information will be used: in a 2023 survey, 71% of non-federal acute-care hospitals reported routinely having outside clinical information available, while 42% reported that their clinicians often used it when treating patients ([Hufstader Gabriel et al., 2024](https://healthit.gov/data/data-briefs/interoperable-exchange-patient-health-information-among-us-hospitals-2023/)). Similarly, in a hospital trial, information exchange did not significantly improve discrepancy correction or reduce adverse drug events overall, illustrating why access must be accompanied by follow-through ([Boockvar et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7651981/)). Measuring the proportion of discrepancies reviewed and appropriately resolved, alongside patients’ experiences taking their medications, would give the team feedback to improve the process over time.
