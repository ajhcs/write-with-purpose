# Structure and purpose

Use this reference when an argument, information-selection problem or substantial revision needs development. Match the planning effort to the work; a brief answer may need no outline.

Identify the question or experience the writing should support and the knowledge its reader reasonably brings. Adjust explanation and order while preserving the author's voice. Treat an audience label as a hypothesis about needs.

Choose material for its contribution to the requested result. Different uses of a record can require different selections. An explicit completeness requirement controls coverage; an actual length requirement controls length. Otherwise, let the necessary content and explanation determine the extent.

For a longer piece, sketch its developing thought as paragraph contributions or topic sentences. Check for missing premises, delayed answers, unsupported jumps and repeated work before expanding it. Place background where the reader needs it. Give secondary detail a place that preserves the main line of thought.

For an assignment, answer the actual question and explain the inference from evidence to conclusion. Apply the submission requirements to that answer.

For an essay or blog, develop an observation or question through a particular object, relationship or scene when that makes the thought intelligible. Reasoning, experience, humor and emotional connection can contribute to the piece. Distinguish personal observation, invented illustration and a claim about a population. Use expression references for their assigned role. Keep an unsettled voice choice small enough for the author to judge.

For a decision or operational explanation, make the choice, reasons and consequences recoverable. A proposed change needs enough description of the existing state and intended state for the reader to understand the change. A procedure also needs executable actions and their conditions. Choose prose, lists or tables for the relationship the reader must recover.

## Reader edit

After drafting, read the passage as a standalone artifact and attempt the reader's actual task using only its text. Look for an inference the reader must reconstruct, an action hidden behind a label, or information that interrupts the answer. For a confusing connection, try retaining the useful framing and exact terms while rewriting the connecting clauses in ordinary language. Where needed, make a concrete edit and read the result again. This can add a necessary link as well as remove excess. An adequate passage can remain unchanged.

For an uncertain unit, try removing, moving or combining it. Compare what the reader can recover from the resulting versions. A qualification may already be expressed by a precise term, bound value, tense or heading. Retain further explanation when it supplies a needed inference. Useful repetition can stabilize a referent, support recall or place a prerequisite at a separate entry point.

Judge the paragraph's flow as well as its individual statements. Combine repeated setup for parallel facts, and give calculations the detail needed for this task and reader. Let the final observation or calculation carry the conclusion when it completes the point; add an ending for a further inference or intended experience.

Check the revised claims against the source separately. An economical edit must retain their necessary conditions, uncertainty and relationships. Complete source coverage answers a different question from whether a selected passage serves its reader.

Use a requested template for the task, with content justified by the source. Keep accepted decisions stable unless new evidence or the person's request reopens them.

Evaluate the actual result: can the reader answer, decide, act or follow the intended experience? Compare concrete alternatives in context. Model critique supplies provisional editorial judgment; author feedback and observed reader performance provide different evidence. Keep conformance, correctness and usefulness judgments distinct.

## Briefs and templates

Use a compact brief when repeated tasks have actual output constraints. Infer settled choices from the request and supplied material; do not make the user fill out a form or expose process notes for a simple edit. A useful brief can specify the requested intervention, audience, required content, word ceiling, approved terminology, source roles and output format.

For example: "Clean up this draft; preserve its sequence and technical terms; do not expand; return prose only." The original supplies the structure. A new explanation might instead require "At most 250 words for a general reader; explain the reported problem and the supported cause; use the supplied terms; include only the requested headings." These are examples of task choices, not default limits or mandatory prose patterns.

Use a supplied template when its sections are required. For other writing, a small outline or an approved example can establish the shape without reusable filler sentences. A vocabulary guide should bind preferred terms to intended concepts and protect necessary names, quotations and technical vocabulary. It should not make unfamiliar words automatically wrong or reward synonym substitution. Test the brief against the delivered writing, including whether constraints were met and whether the requested meaning survives.
