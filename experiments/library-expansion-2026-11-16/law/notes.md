# Law and public policy retrieval candidates

Search and verification snapshot: 2026-11-16. These are metadata-based candidates, not measured writing samples. No article body was downloaded. Primary selection is through 2014; 2015–2019 works are flagged as a separate supplement in JSON. OpenAlex live records and Crossref API title comparison supply bibliographic verification. Crossref matched JSTOR registration DOIs on historical law-review works; these are electronic identifiers and do not establish that there is no other publisher DOI. OpenAlex's scholarly citation count is an imperfect provider-specific discovery signal, not judicial citation, readership, a ranking in all literature, or proof of a writing model. All classifications remain provisional until the actual text is inspected.

A jurisdictionally meaningful subject distinction matters: the three Campbell review and three protocol candidates are evidence syntheses about **policing and crime policy**, not reviews of judicial doctrine. A review of a named book is an article about that book, not the book itself. Review [Solum](https://scholarship.law.tamu.edu/facscholar/2481/) and [Grewal](https://harvardlawreview.org/print/vol-128/the-laws-of-capitalism/) have original journal venues and pagination supplied by the primary journal or author's institutional repository, because OpenAlex led with repositories. The Cox review record includes the book author as an apparent coauthor in OpenAlex; the review author is Archibald Cox. All titles and publication details still merit source-text checks before corpus measurement.

The Crossref title for the 2004 Donohue and Levitt article omits the subtitle present in the OpenAlex work record. Its DOI is deliberately withheld rather than asserting an exact identity match; the journal landing page and full citation in the live OpenAlex record provide the retrieval locator. OpenAlex often lists a single starting page as both ends; the duplicate end is suppressed. A lone page means **starting page only**. The Harvard Journal issue identifies the Solum review as 127 Harv. L. Rev. 2464–2497 and Grewal as 128 Harv. L. Rev. 626–667.

[HeinOnline's most-cited ranking](https://access.heinonline.com/HOL/scholarly-impact-ranking/comprehensive-articles) was a discovery source, not a verified global ranking. Its previously retained snapshot has separate article citations and accesses; do not mix those measures or infer judicial counts from OpenAlex. The local historical discovery and full-text ledgers should be deduplicated before requesting copies. Already-retained official Heller brief is excluded.

For unfilled forms, coverage.json identifies the outstanding search rather than declaring the form nonexistent. Practice manuals and government reports can be valuable but require historical edition, author, intended audience, exact document function and retrieval verification; a legal advice memo is not automatically a decision memo. Judicial opinions and case commentary are not clinical case reports. The [OLC practice guidance](https://www.justice.gov/olc/best-practices-olc-legal-advice-and-written-opinions) is an alternate lead only, with no unsupported claim of exceptional readership. Campbell [2019 disorder policing review](https://www.campbellcollaboration.org/review/disorder-policing-systematic-review/) and Harvard [The Life of the Law](https://hls.harvard.edu/bibliography/the-life-of-the-law-book-review/) are alternate leads, not silently substituted candidates.

## Second pass: expanded forms

The follow-up added first-person legal reflections, primary-described methods articles, actual syntheses of research, dated CRS explanations, public policy proposals, and declassified action memoranda. [Cunningham](https://repository.law.umich.edu/mlr/vol87/iss8/12/) explicitly frames his narrative as his personal search and client experiences; [Edwards](https://repository.law.umich.edu/mjlr/vol13/iss1/2/) is a reflective graduation speech. [White](https://repository.law.umich.edu/lqnotes/vol40/iss2/10/) is a 1997 adapted version of a 1996 lecture; do not treat both as independent examples. These reflections are selected for identifiable form and institutional provenance, not established high readership. A 2015 comparative-law literature review joins the separately marked supplement.

The decision memos are discrete documents transcribed in the government-published *Foreign Relations of the United States* series; the work to retrieve is the named memo or transmitted action memo, not its collected book volume. The three policy proposals are author-originated Brookings/Hamilton Project publications; their selection is an institutional signal, not a claim that the proposals succeeded or were widely read. Three CRS explanations are specifically dated government reports preserved in the University of North Texas government-documents collection.

The practitioner sources are a different era cohort: two Minnesota court information sheets, the Duke/Berkeley research tutorial, and three Cornell Wex entries have no established original publication date. They are current-version exploratory examples and must not be counted as historical pre-2015 documents. Wex's listed last-review date is not its original publication date. The Osborne 2013 item concerns instruction and includes exercises; full-text inspection must decide whether it is a tutorial rather than scholarship about teaching. A third standalone tutorial has not been verified. A targeted search for pre-2020 legal/public-policy scoping reviews did not produce three verified candidates; this remains an active search gap, not a claim that such work does not exist. We did not reinterpret legal case commentary as a clinical case report.
