# Headline attention: evidence review

These papers support testing title choices in context, not a rule to maximize clicks or prefer vague, negative, long, or short wording. All three use the same historical Upworthy archive. Their subsets and methods differ, and their exact overlap is unknown; they are not three independent replications. The [structured review](headlines-attention.json) records the full sample, results, source-page locators and proposed checks.

Randomized exposure supports causal comparisons between the **complete headline/image packages actually tested**. The words, lexical features and the average concreteness of alternatives were not independently randomized. Their regression coefficients and interactions are therefore model-dependent feature associations, even when the papers discuss them causally. Clickthrough measures selection of a preview, not comprehension, meaningful reading, satisfaction, author voice or writing quality.

## P04: concreteness and curiosity gaps

Aubin Le Quéré and Matias, *When curiosity gaps backfire* (2025), [DOI 10.1038/s41598-024-81575-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-81575-9), is a registered multilevel meta-analysis of field tests. Its confirmatory analysis retained **8,977 same-image bundles and 35,910 headline observations** after filtering, including repeated headlines; it started with 17,867 tests and 85,830 packages (PDF p. 6). Splitting original tests by image means bundles need not all be independent original experiments. A model found a negative interaction between within-test headline concreteness and the mean of alternatives (β = −0.058, 95% CI −0.091 to −0.025; p. 7, Table 2). The estimated slope was positive below a test mean of 2.58 and negative above 3.06; the authors report those regions covered 8.7% and 50.9% of retained tests respectively (pp. 8–9). These are model-dependent, sample-specific conditional comparisons, not universal cutoffs or a measured optimal title for a given reader.

The lexical score correlated with average human concreteness judgments (r = 0.61 for 320 selected headlines; pp. 4–5), but is not a direct measure of how much useful information a reader gets. The model omits headlines containing an unrecognized longer word, including some novel terms (p. 4), a particular concern for technical titles. Actual reader knowledge or curiosity was not measured (p. 9). The paper's p. 5 gives CTR as impressions divided by clicks, contrary to its correct definition on p. 3; p. 6 calls the scored confirmatory packages “pilot” headlines, and p. 8 reverses the high/low wording in an example. These should be resolved before reproducing numeric examples.

## P07: headline feature prediction

Gligorić and colleagues, *Linguistic effects on news headline success* (2023), [DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0281682](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0281682), trained on 5,048 pilot pairs and evaluated 24,333 held-out pairs selected for statistically distinguishable CTR within matched article/image/week (pp. 8–10). The feature model predicted the winner with **54.42% accuracy** against a balanced 50% baseline, 99% CI 53.6%–55.2% (p. 10). Unique headlines within selected pairs do not prove independence between pairs from the same experiment. Selection on statistical significance also limits generalization to all headline comparisons.

The confirmatory regression associated negative-emotion word presence and longer character count with winning, but did not support the preregistered negative association for positive-emotion words or Flesch score at p < 0.01 (pp. 10–12). Indefinite articles and some pronoun indicators were associated with success, while the second-person hypothesis was not supported at that threshold (p. 12). Those indicators are imperfect proxies for specificity, readership, or tone; the authors acknowledge that alternatives changed multiple linguistic variables at once (pp. 16–17). Their own model's modest held-out accuracy does not justify automated title preference. The p. 12 prose and Table 3 disagree on the definite-article p-value; no disputed value is used here.

## P13: negative words

Robertson and colleagues, *Negativity drives online news consumption* (2023), [DOI 10.1038/s41562-023-01538-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-023-01538-4), analyzed 22,743 confirmatory experiments before filtering. After filtering, it reports **12,448 experiments, 53,699 headline variants, over 205 million impressions and 2,778,124 clicks** (p. 3; Table 3 p. 4). The multilevel model adjusted for length, a complexity proxy and platform age, and a varying-slope sensitivity model retained the same sentiment directions (pp. 4, 8).

The authors estimate that an additional dictionary-classified negative word in a headline of average length corresponds to a **2.3% relative increase in CTR**, not a 2.3-percentage-point increase (p. 3). It is a fitted feature estimate, not a randomized single-word substitution. Their positive-word association was negative, unlike P07's unsupported positive-word hypothesis; samples, selection, score construction and estimands differ, so the difference is not a clean contradiction test. Discrete-emotion results were mixed: sadness was positive, fear and joy negative, while anger was not significant and its equivalence test was inconclusive (p. 5). Dictionary labels do not establish a reader's felt emotion (pp. 6–8). Images sometimes varied with headlines; the authors report a sensitivity analysis excluding image experiments (pp. 4, 7). The figure caption's 53,669 headline count differs from the 53,699 in the text and table, and one complexity coefficient has an apparent sign error (p. 4).

## Implications for this plugin

The existing [writing skill](/workspace/repos/builderbob/write-with-purpose/skills/write-with-purpose/SKILL.md) already puts the author's purpose, accurate meaning, and voice first, and its [structure reference](/workspace/repos/builderbob/write-with-purpose/skills/write-with-purpose/references/structure-and-purpose.md) asks what the reader needs. Reaffirm those choices. When a title or heading is in scope, a candidate improvement is to show a small set of faithful alternatives and explain what each reveals or leaves open for the intended reader and form. A reflective essay can deliberately defer meaning; an API reference entry may require an exact identifier. Neither should be judged by an Upworthy score.

Mechanically check declared length limits, explicit title strings and heading structure. A person must judge whether a title accurately represents the contribution, whether omitted information misleads, and whether tone and voice fit. A genuinely negative event can warrant a negative title; adding negative words solely to increase clicks is not supported as an author's-purpose or quality rule. Existing local pattern and function-calibration experiments also do not establish a fixed section sequence or a validated automatic semantic classifier.

The papers identify an [analysis and sentence-concreteness package](https://github.com/maubinle/sentence_concreteness) (P04 p. 10), [analysis code and data](https://osf.io/uscpf/) (P13 p. 9), and a text-statistics library (P07 reference 57). These are leads only: no code was installed or run, and licenses and dependencies were not independently reviewed. Any adoption would need a scoped terms review and validation against the plugin's actual forms and readers. An author-controlled, source-grounded comparison workflow is a plausible differentiator; a controlled reader study against relevant outcomes would be needed to demonstrate an advantage.
