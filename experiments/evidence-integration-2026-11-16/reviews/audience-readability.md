# Audience, readability and scientific writing

16 November 2026. The three papers support adapting language to a particular audience and testing the result, but they assess different outcomes. None validates a universal readability grade, a ban on technical terms, a paragraph-function classifier, or a rule to maximize clicks. Full paper-level evidence, numeric denominators, extraction issues, and proposed rule records are in [audience-readability.json](audience-readability.json). PDF page numbers below refer to the supplied extracted files.

**P01 — Shulman et al. (2020), [The Effects of Jargon on Processing Fluency, Self-Perceptions, and Scientific Engagement](https://doi.org/10.1177/0261927X20902177).** This randomized experiment assigned 650 U.S. panel participants to jargon versus explanatory wording and optional mouseover definitions versus none. Each read three short science-technology passages; the jargon treatment included ten technical terms per paragraph. On a 1–7 self-reported scale, mean processing fluency was 4.57 with jargon and 5.27 without it (pp. 6–10). Definition availability had neither a statistically significant main effect nor interaction with jargon. This is evidence about optional mouseovers under these conditions, not evidence that an inline explanation cannot help: 109 of 330 offered the feature reported using it, and use was self-selected (pp. 7, 14–15). The article labels that fraction 31%, although 109/330 is approximately 33%.

The experiment did **not** measure objective comprehension or actual engagement behavior. Unadjusted differences in self-schema and four engagement outcomes were not statistically significant. The proposed sequential mediation uses nonrandomized, cross-sectional mediators and outcomes; the authors explicitly acknowledge that the pathway is not established as causal (pp. 10, 12, 14). A causal effect of the randomized wording on perceived fluency is more defensible than a causal claim about the whole chain.

**P05 — Pancer et al. (2019), [How Readability Shapes Social Media Engagement](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcpy.1073).** A field analysis of 4,064 Humans of New York Facebook posts reported associations between a Dale–Chall index and actual likes, comments, and shares; regression tables use 4,063 observations (pp. 2–5). It controlled measured features but cannot exclude audience reach, content or other confounding. A separate randomized experiment with 236 participants changed word familiarity and length in a fictional running-shoe post; it measured reported willingness to like, comment, or share, not actual behavior (pp. 4–7). Simpler wording increased mean liking intention, with an interaction by length. For short texts, differences in commenting and sharing intentions were not statistically significant. Neither result defines an optimal length.

The Twitter follow-up used the same HONY source and potentially overlapping stories; it is not an independent population of stories. The article does not state its sample count in the supplied main text. The hypothesized fluency–affect pathway remains a mediation model rather than a randomized test of each mediator. Reading time did not prove that participants abandoned the longer complex text (p. 7). The authors themselves note that familiar wording depends on community and that simplifying can sacrifice precision (p. 8).

**P09 — Ryba et al. (2021), [Better Writing in Scientific Publications Builds Reader Confidence and Understanding](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.714321).** In a randomized experiment, 170 third-year science undergraduates read different-topic versions of three scientific abstracts, each prepared at four levels that bundled nine style components. Readability and confidence were self-reported; understanding was assessed with two multiple-choice questions per reading. Models included topic and reader effects. The comprehension model had 628 binary item responses from 143 participants, with an odds ratio of 1.08 per unit of the composite style axis (95% CI 1.01–1.15); the model does not establish the separate effect of acronyms, hedges, punctuation, or any other one component (pp. 2–6).

The unadjusted comprehension percentages in Table 2 are **not monotonic**: 57.87%, 39.47%, 40.79%, and 47.95% from easiest to hardest. The modeled result adjusts for topic and repeated readers and should be interpreted on its odds scale. No interaction with topic or effect for English as a second language was detected, but the authors caution that their sample was not designed for the latter comparison. These null results do not establish equivalence (pp. 4–6).

Two internal discrepancies limit numeric reuse: Table 2 gives easiest/hardest confidence as 62.36%/48.78%, while the results prose gives 58.3%/44.1%; later discussion returns to about 62%/49%. Also, the discussion says all three outcomes were self-reported, while the methods and results specify scored multiple-choice understanding. The latter design is the more specific account; neither discrepancy was silently resolved (pp. 3–6).

## Implications for the existing work

- **Audience and terminology:** Reaffirm the writer skill's instruction to use familiar, exact words while preserving precise technical distinctions. When an audience may be unfamiliar with a necessary term, make its meaning recoverable in the reading context and test the choice with relevant readers. Optional access to a definition is not proof that it was used or understood. For specialists, the technical term can itself be the clearest wording. This refines [write-with-purpose](/workspace/repos/builderbob/write-with-purpose/skills/write-with-purpose/SKILL.md) and its [language reference](/workspace/repos/builderbob/write-with-purpose/skills/write-with-purpose/references/language-and-meaning.md); it does not require replacing the skill.
- **Preserve epistemic force:** P09's tested bundles included different hedge counts, but they cannot identify the effect of hedge removal. Retain uncertainty and conditions warranted by the evidence; a more confident sentence that changes the claim is not an acceptable simplification. Checks can flag removed modals or glossary entries for review, but determining meaning and audience need remains semantic.
- **Keep outcomes distinct:** A readability formula can report its vocabulary list, tokenizer, and count, but cannot certify familiarity, comprehension or quality. Feelings of fluency, multiple-choice performance, author satisfaction, source fidelity, and online engagement need separate evidence. The accepted F01–F09 features are a measurement framework, not default scores or targets.
- **Test a plausible advantage:** Audience-aware drafting that preserves technical distinctions, uncertainty, author voice, and complete meaning is a hypothesis worth testing with held-out texts and representative readers. The current fresh-paragraph calibration found exact label-set agreement of 21/36 for original guidance and 18/36 for the revised guidance, with no semantic gold; it establishes neither better comprehension nor improved semantic accuracy. Submitted evidence quotes were not complete realization spans, so no function-length profiles follow.

The downloaded [scientific crosswalk](/workspace/repos/builderbob/write-with-purpose/experiments/dataset-adoption-2026-11-16/reviews/scientific-crosswalk.md) and source codebooks offer examples of attribution, topic and sentence roles for constructing evaluation cases. Those are different annotation layers from reader comprehension, and no source label directly supplies paragraph-function gold. No relevant reader-outcome GitHub dataset or code from these papers was independently verified. Reader tests, source-fidelity review, author acceptance and audience-specific sampling remain new work; no advantage has yet been demonstrated.
