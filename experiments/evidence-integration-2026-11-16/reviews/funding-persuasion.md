# Funding, persuasion and writing: evidence review

These studies do **not** establish that a wording change causes a proposal to be funded. They do challenge blanket advice to remove adjectives, technical terms or complexity. The defensible editorial distinction is between **well-supported emphasis**, which can communicate significance, and an **unsupported claim**, which needs evidence or qualification. Existing voice and source-fidelity rules already protect that distinction.

## Kickstarter: retrospective prediction is not persuasion

Mitra and Gilbert, “[The Language that Gets People to Give: Phrases that Predict Success on Kickstarter](https://doi.org/10.1145/2531602.2531656)” (2014), modeled 45,810 ended campaigns scraped in August 2012; 23,604 were funded and 22,206 were not (PDF p. 3). The model combined 20,391 selected unigrams through trigrams with 59 controls. The authors report ten-fold cross-validation error of 17.03% for controls alone and 2.4% with phrases (p. 5, Table 2). Figure 3 distinguishes 2.41% cross-validation error from 2.24% prediction error on an 80/20 train/test split at alpha = 1 (p. 5); Figure 4 and adjacent prose do not clearly identify their metric basis (p. 6).

Those figures are retrospective. Project homepages could be edited before the after-deadline scrape, and the controls include counts of updates and comments and featuring on pages including “Most Funded” (pp. 4–5). Examples include language acknowledging progress already achieved (p. 10). This creates serious potential for temporal leakage or reverse association; cross-validation does not fix data that were unavailable at the intended prediction time. It is not evidence of prelaunch accuracy or the causal effect of inserting a phrase. The paper also explicitly disclaims causal claims (p. 11). Its examples of clear offers, rewards and audience value are useful **questions to consider when factually applicable**, not phrases to copy or guarantees to make.

The paper's “58.56%” is not ordinary variance explained. The stated increment in deviance, 37,162.10, is 58.56% of the null deviance of 63,463.47; relative to the controls-only deviance of 37,525.52, it is about 99.03% (p. 5). The prose's denominator is unclear. The text also says its constant baseline predicts the not-funded class, although funded is the majority (pp. 3 and 6). Treat the quantitative result as the authors' retrospective model report pending code and timing audit. Authors identify phrase files on p. 11; availability and rights have not been checked here.

## Biomedical grants: positive association is not proof that hype works

Qiu and colleagues, “[Use of Promotional Language in Grant Applications and Grant Success](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.48696)” (2024), studied full biomedical applications from the Novo Nordisk Foundation (NNF, 2015–2022) and NIH applications by Northwestern faculty (2007–2019). NNF funded 1,579 of 9,096 applications and NIH funded 481 of 2,439 (p. 2). This is a cross-sectional study, with covariate-adjusted logistic regressions and a 139-term dictionary; no wording was randomized.

The reported adjusted odds ratios for a one percentage-point increase in promotional-word fraction were **1.47** (95% CI 1.25–1.71) for NNF and **1.51** (1.10–2.11) for NIH (pp. 3, 5). Figure 2 prints 1.46 for NNF, a rounding/reporting discrepancy (p. 6). These are odds ratios, not probabilities or an expected increase from editing a particular grant. Mean fractions in funded versus unfunded applications were 1.02% versus 0.98% for NNF and 0.93% versus 0.89% for NIH (p. 4). The authors report five-fold cross-validation without a main-text held-out accuracy estimate, and request further causal research (pp. 6, 8).

The dictionary counts language that can draw positive attention; it cannot determine whether any particular claim is accurate, exaggerated or necessary. The association qualifies a blanket prohibition on positive evaluative words. It does **not** justify adding promotional vocabulary, deleting qualifications, promising unverified outcomes, or treating funding as proof of scientific merit. Agency, field, applicant and selection differences constrain transfer to business proposals or decision memos. Data access and supplements remain separate follow-ups (pp. 2, 9–10).

## Early-career grants: context, section and review stage matter

Van den Besselaar and Mom, “[The effect of writing style on success in grant applications](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joi.2022.101257)” (2022), examined a single early-career scheme and funding round in 2014. Of 3,207 submissions, 3,030 applicants consented; 2,900 remained after text-processing exclusions and 2,532 after field and bibliometric restrictions (pp. 4–5). Their first-stage project-description tables use **2,493** cases; stage-two tables use **677**, selected through the prior stage (pp. 7–8). External review and an oral presentation also intervene. Humanities, law and qualitative social science applications were excluded from final analysis; English proficiency is approximated at country level (p. 5).

In the adjusted first-stage project-score model, certainty-word coefficient B was 0.043 (95% CI 0.022–0.063), while tentative-word B was 0.002 (−0.020–0.023), p = 0.885 (p. 7, Table 3; N = 2,493). The progression model gave certainty OR 1.180 (1.070–1.303), tentative OR 0.986 (0.890–1.093), and causal-word OR 1.050 (0.952–1.158; p. 8, Table 5; same N). The tentative result is not proof of equivalence, and certainty-word counts do not justify strengthening factual certainty.

For the same first-stage project-score model, word count and sentence length had positive linear terms and negative squared terms (p. 7, Table 3); that conditional, panel-standardized quadratic relationship is no transferable word or sentence target. The common-word measure also conflates vocabulary familiarity with technical content and readability (p. 3). Stage-two project-score and final-decision results differ: common words were associated with project score (B −0.030, p = 0.036) but not demonstrably with funding (OR 0.863, 95% CI 0.707–1.053; pp. 8–9, Tables 6–7; N = 677). The authors' blanket statement about stage-two results goes beyond the decision table. Table 10 also reports a significant association for tentative words and first-stage project score in abstracts (B 0.024, p = 0.025; p. 10, N = 2,493), contrary to the discussion's broad abstract-null wording. These are proxy associations, not demonstrated text quality or effects of edits.

The final-score model's Nagelkerke pseudo-R² is 0.191 with all covariates and 0.146 with linguistic variables alone; for first-stage progression the values are 0.131 and 0.100 (pp. 7–8, Tables 4–5; N = 2,493). These are pseudo-R² model-fit statistics, not proportions of ordinary variance or external prediction accuracy. The paper reports no held-out prospective evaluation. Its own discussion warns that polished self-presentation can oversell and introduce evaluation bias (p. 11).

## Candidate, reversible rule tests

The machine-readable review contains four scoped candidates; none changes an accepted rule:

- **FP-C01 — evidence-linked proposal emphasis.** When the author requests a proposal or decision memo, show unresolved claim-to-source links or gaps. A reviewer judges whether benefit, cost, risk and request are relevant and supported. A speculative benefit can remain when marked as such; no default checklist of required sections.
- **FP-C02 — strength-of-claim review.** Mechanically flag changed modals, negations, numbers and superlatives as candidates. Semantically compare each change with the source and author intent; retain necessary uncertainty. A stronger claim is acceptable when evidence supports it.
- **FP-C03 — audience-sensitive clarity.** Show only requested counts and enforce only declared limits. Compare alternatives for meaning, precision, readability and voice; retain necessary technical terms and complex qualifications.
- **FP-C04 — contextual decision support.** Offer optional decision, options and support prompts when the task requires them. Judge whether they help the actual reader; do not impose a universal order or claim funding gains.

These refine or reaffirm [drafting guidance](../../../skills/write-with-purpose/SKILL.md), [claim fidelity](../../../skills/write-with-purpose/references/concepts-and-claims.md), [uncertainty and force](../../../skills/write-with-purpose/references/language-and-meaning.md), and [targeted editing](../../../skills/edit-with-purpose/SKILL.md). Mechanical alerts are review cues, not semantic or source-validation verdicts. The calibration experiment's original **21/36** versus revised **18/36** exact-set agreement did not establish better function coding, so these papers do not validate new paragraph labels or a function quota. Imported section roles, speech acts and discourse relations remain distinct layers. A source-linked, author-controlled comparison is a plausible product hypothesis; no measured competitive advantage has been demonstrated.
