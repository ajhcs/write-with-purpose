# Narrative, style and impact: evidence review

16 November 2026. The three supplied papers examine sharing, citation counts, and perceived importance—not direct measures of writing quality, comprehension, author voice or task success. The full structured review is [narrative-style-impact.json](narrative-style-impact.json). Page numbers below refer to physical PDF pages, which correspond to the printed page numbers in these supplied versions.

## P06: Berger and Milkman, *What Makes Online Content Viral?*

[Journal of Marketing Research, 2012, DOI 10.1509/jmr.10.0353](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.10.0353). The supplied ahead-of-print copy carries © 2011; the publisher identifies the 2012 issue. This is an observational field study and three randomized story-version experiments, not a meta-analysis.

The authors tracked **6,956 New York Times homepage articles** from August–November 2008; 20% appeared on the most-emailed list. A random 2,566 were manually assessed for specific emotions and other qualities, by three independent raters per construct. Uncoded scores were set to the standardized mean with an indicator in the model; the authors report similar results restricted to coded articles. The field model includes day effects and controls for position and exposure, among other variables (PDF pp. 3–4, 6–9). The outcome is appearing on an emailed ranking, not a direct probability that an individual reader shares.

In the main adjusted model, one standard deviation more rated anger corresponded to **34% higher odds** of list entry; awe corresponded to 30% higher odds (pp. 8–9, Table 5 and discussion). These are conditional associations, not percentage-point probability changes or proof that adding anger causes sharing. Positivity and arousal are different dimensions: in this setting, high-arousal anger, anxiety and awe were positively associated with the outcome, whereas low-arousal sadness was negatively associated. This does not establish that all negative writing is viral or that emotional intensity is always useful.

Randomly assigned story versions produced higher **self-reported** sharing likelihood for higher versus lower amusement (3.97 vs 2.92 on a seven-point scale, N=49) and anger (5.71 vs 3.37, N=45); high-sadness versus low-sadness versions yielded 2.39 vs 3.80 (N=47; pp. 9–11). These manipulations also changed substantive details, such as airline damage and response or the cause of an injury. Assignment supports an effect of the presented versions on stated intentions; arousal mediation was modeled, not itself randomized. No study tests whether a true story or its illustration helps a reader understand, or whether virality is desirable for the author's task.

## P08: Hillier, Kelly and Klinger, *Narrative Style Influences Citation Frequency in Climate Change Science*

[PLOS ONE, 2016, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0167983](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0167983). This is an observational study of 732 abstracts after quality control from 802 retrieved articles, spanning 19 journals and 2009–2010. Articles with zero citations were excluded. Seven initial independent crowd assessments per abstract came from 155 contributors. The six narrativity indicators included setting, first person, sensory language, conjunctions, sentence connectivity and appeal; they extend beyond narration as events through time (pp. 2–5).

The composite index had **R²=.05** with log citations; journal-level mean index and impact factor had R²=.62. Journal identity alone accounted for R²=.43, and a selected model using year, index, author count and impact factor accounted for R²=.41 (pp. 5–8). The selected model uses impact factor rather than individual journal identity, so journal selection and editorial context remain major confounds. Conjunctions, connectivity and sensory indicators showed small positive correlations; setting and narrative perspective were not significant in the listed univariate tests (p=.36 and .32; p. 7, Table 1). The study did not test cognitive mechanisms (p. 9). Citation frequency does not establish reading, comprehension or recall, despite causal language in parts of the paper.

The official PLOS supplements are now available and inspected: [instructions](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article/file?type=supplementary&id=info:doi/10.1371/journal.pone.0167983.s003), [six questions](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article/file?type=supplementary&id=info:doi/10.1371/journal.pone.0167983.s004), [raw judgments](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article/file?type=supplementary&id=info:doi/10.1371/journal.pone.0167983.s009), [analysis script](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article/file?type=supplementary&id=info:doi/10.1371/journal.pone.0167983.s008) and [journal data](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article/file?type=supplementary&id=info:doi/10.1371/journal.pone.0167983.s010). The raw CSV contains **5,614 judgment rows across 802 unit IDs, seven per unit**; these are pre-quality-control records, distinct from the paper’s 732 final abstracts. The instrument asks three yes/no questions—place/time, author self-reference and appeal—and three counts: sensory/emotional words, cause/contrast/time connectives, and links to the preceding sentence. These broad measures do not equate to our `narrate` function or identify paragraph-function spans. The R script was read as text only: it groups by unit, filters judgments by the majority answer to the first-person question, aggregates and filters by year. It was not executed, and the results have not been reproduced. The data provide a potential multi-rater methods resource, not additional causal evidence. Source-text and contributor metadata remain in the private cache; only aggregate counts and instrument descriptions are retained here. ## P12: Boghrati, Berger and Packard, *Style, content, and the success of ideas*

[Journal of Consumer Psychology, 2023, DOI 10.1002/jcpy.1346](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcpy.1346). The observational component analyzes 28,774 1990–2018 articles in selected journals across five social sciences. It models Google Scholar citations using topic mixtures and other content proxies, journal/year and other controls, then adds nine function-word rates. In the negative-binomial comparison, adjusted R² rose from **.208 to .217**; in the neural model predicted R² rose from .209 to .234 (pp. 3–5, Table 1). The authors’ 4–11% refers to a **share of explained variance**, not the percentage of all variance in citations. The association is not an experiment, and topic controls do not exhaust content quality, novelty or author selection; the authors acknowledge the quality/novelty gap (p. 11).

Exploratory relationships varied by section. First-person usage was positively associated with citations in front matter (b=.076, p<.001), negatively in methods/results (b=−.029, p=.016), and not significantly associated in conclusions (b=.007, p=.29). More prepositions in the front were associated negatively (b=−.015), but in the middle positively (b=.027); article frequency in the middle was not significant (b=−.002, p=.66; pp. 7–8). These are model coefficients, not validated rewriting targets or direct reader-comprehension measures. Grammar supplies agency, negation, time and relationships, even when function-word frequencies serve as statistical style features. Removing words or changing tense can change the content being communicated.

The randomized one-sentence experiments assess perceptions rather than actual citations. In N=185, “we” versus a non-personal subject yielded importance ratings 6.15 vs 5.76 and expected impact 5.97 vs 5.39 on seven-point scales (p. 9). In N=234, present versus past auxiliary wording yielded importance 5.97 vs 5.60 and expected impact 5.69 vs 5.20 (pp. 9–10). These show responses to those versions, not that changing a whole paper's voice or tense raises citations; the wording also changes agency or temporal framing. The Study 4 results report F(1,200) for perceived impact despite N=234 and F(1,232) for importance, and the mediation paragraph incorrectly calls the manipulation personal voice. Preserve these discrepancies pending source clarification rather than silently repairing them.

## Implications for our own harness

- **Reaffirm author-controlled narrative and examples (NS01).** The existing `narrate` and `illustrate` functions are useful options when a supplied experience or concrete case develops the author's thought. Sharing or citations do not justify making stories mandatory, inventing an opening, or treating an analogy as empirical proof. The accepted Cole voice reference already shows an example developing an argument without making that pattern universal.
- **Reaffirm meaning and voice protection (NS02).** The current writing and editing skills preserve actor, tense, qualification, technical vocabulary and effective wording. P12 provides a reason to consider voice and section context, not to optimize pronouns or delete prepositions. Offer alternatives only when they preserve supported temporal and evidential meaning; the author decides whether they fit.
- **Reaffirm target links (NS03).** `support` needs a claim and relevant reasoning; `interpret` needs an inference grounded in the material. A popular anecdote may illustrate a point without proving it. GUM `explanation-evidence` and argument annotations provide existing links and boundary examples, but do not establish whether a passage's evidence is adequate.
- **Do not set a popularity or readability default (NS04).** A user may explicitly prioritize reach, but citation, sharing and word-form proxies cannot replace their purpose, factual fidelity, technical precision or learning goals. Existing foundations already separate observed distributions from author-selected targets and declared limits.

A source adapter that preserves original graph labels, linked spans, uncertainty and author decisions is reusable common infrastructure. An author-controlled workflow that contrasts meaningful alternatives while protecting voice is a plausible differentiator, not a demonstrated advantage. The local 36-paragraph calibration found 21/36 versus 18/36 exact-set agreement and no demonstrated improvement; the imported crosswalk is not reader-outcome gold. Validate proposed changes using independent readers and direct measures such as comprehension, task completion, fidelity and author acceptance, rather than inferring success from citation or sharing.
