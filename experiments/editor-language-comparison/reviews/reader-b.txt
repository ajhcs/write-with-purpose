Q2. Was a medication change ordered?

Status: answered

Evidence: “A dose reduction was suggested during the review, but no medication order has been entered.”

Missing, unclear or outside scope: The text supports absence of an entered order; it does not establish events outside the documentation. Its wording is less explicitly bounded to the supplied record than B.

Q3. What symptom occurred the previous evening, and what is known about its cause?

Status: answered

Answer: Mira reported nearly fainting the previous evening and did not report losing consciousness. The cause remains unconfirmed in the record.

Evidence: “Mira reported nearly fainting the previous evening, but she did not report losing consciousness.” | “The cause of the symptoms remains unconfirmed; no cause has yet been established in the record.”

Missing, unclear or outside scope: The actual cause is intentionally unresolved, not omitted evidence that the reader should supply. 'Did not report' is not proof that loss of consciousness did not occur.

information selection: All required clinical distinctions are retained. The statements that different positions are different positions, and that a suggestion is not an order, add no operational distinction beyond the immediately preceding wording.

findability: Readings, suggestion and symptom history are recoverable, but extra sentences separate the relevant facts and lengthen scanning.

voice: The neutral reporting language fits the clinical context. The tautological explanations make the voice sound more pedantic than useful to the intended clinician.

clarity of connections: 'But' clearly connects a suggested dose reduction to absence of an entered order and near-fainting to the lack of a reported loss of consciousness. The extra explanations repeat these connections rather than clarify them.

useful explanation: Posture, the two-minute timing, the review context and causal uncertainty are useful. The two explanatory sentences do not explain a mechanism, implication or documentation status.

unnecessary effort: The reader must pass over two full restatements and a further restatement of uncertainty. None is difficult to parse, but each costs attention without resolving ambiguity.

brief coverage: Meets substantive coverage: positions, timing, suggestion/order distinction and uncertainty are present; no diagnosis or new recommendation is added. Source-relative voice and targetedness remain unassessable.

CASE 04

Brief requirement: A short first-person blog passage must retain the concrete confusing-bus-stop scene and existing voice, adding no history, statistics or invented events. A general lesson is optional only if it adds something beyond the scene.

VERSION A

Q1. Why did the narrator go to the wrong stop?

Status: answered

Answer: The narrator followed the large blue sign to the usual shelter, which was fenced off for roadworks.

Evidence: “I missed the replacement bus stop because I followed the large blue sign.” | “It sent me to the usual shelter, which was fenced off for roadworks.”

Missing, unclear or outside scope: None. The text does not say why the large sign was wrong, and no such explanation is needed.

Q2. What did the smaller notice add, and how did it also mislead?

Status: answered

Answer: It gave the temporary stop's address, but its arrow pointed back toward the fenced-off shelter.

Evidence: “A smaller notice at the corner gave the temporary stop’s address, but its arrow pointed back toward the shelter.”

Missing, unclear or outside scope: The actual street address is not provided, but its exact value is incidental to the brief and to understanding the contradictory directions.

Q3. How does the passage end, and what work does the ending do?

Status: answered

Answer: It ends with the narrator walking the block twice before a driver waves them over. That concrete action supplies a modest resolution after the conflicting directions and keeps the anecdote in its first-person scene.

Evidence: “I walked the block twice before a driver waved me over.”

Missing, unclear or outside scope: The resolving effect is a reader interpretation. The text does not explicitly say the narrator boarded a bus or reached the temporary stop, so those events must not be invented.

Reading judgments:

flow: Each sentence develops the misdirection: large sign, unusable shelter, contradictory smaller notice, repeated walk and driver's intervention. The last action completes the small narrative arc.

information selection: The sign's size and color, roadworks, notice/address/arrow conflict and repeated walking all contribute concrete causes or consequences. No extra abstraction is needed.

findability: Both causes of confusion and the outcome can be found immediately in a four-sentence sequence.

voice: Direct, slightly rueful first-person narration. The unelaborated driver detail lets the scene carry its tone.

clarity of connections: 'Because' states the initial cause; 'but' exposes the smaller notice's conflict; 'before' establishes the ending's sequence.

useful explanation: The scene itself explains why the narrator became confused. The concrete contradiction between an address and an arrow does more work than a general statement about communication would.

unnecessary effort: Low. No material ambiguity requires the reader to fill in a missing cause.

brief coverage: Fits the concrete first-person scene and adds no history, statistics or event beyond it. Omitting a general lesson is expressly allowed by the brief.
